# Learning science for a 7–8-year-old learning a language through a self-paced app plus school

Evidence grading used here:
- **Strong**: several meta-analyses, including child samples, with consistent results.
- **Moderate**: meta-analytic support mostly from older learners, or a few child studies.
- **Limited**: single studies, theory or expert frameworks, or adult-only evidence applied to children.

No search in this round targeted Chinese-character (hanzi) learning specifically. The findings below are general or come from alphabetic/EFL settings; see the Gaps sections.

## 1. Retrieval practice and spacing (SRS) in children

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (quizzing) and spacing reliably improve long-term retention in primary-age children. The **evidence is strong** for both, but classroom effects at elementary level are smaller (g ≈ 0.33) than lab effects (g ≈ 0.6–0.7). Spacing helps most at delayed tests. Expanding schedules (the SRS logic) showed an advantage over equal spacing for children at a 24-hour delay in one line of studies, so **evidence for expanding schedules specifically is limited**.

### Cited Findings
- Adesope et al. (2017) meta-analysis: 272 independent effects from 118 experiments; overall testing effect g = 0.61. By level, secondary g = 0.83, primary g = 0.64, postsecondary g = 0.60 — [Search summary of Adesope et al. / retrievalpractice.org guide](https://pdf.retrievalpractice.org/MetaAnalysisGuide.pdf); [ResearchGate: Rethinking the Use of Tests](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315706448_Rethinking_the_Use_of_Tests_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Practice_Testing)
- A meta-analysis summarised in the retrievalpractice.org guide reports d = 0.70 (CI 0.63–0.78) across 200+ comparisons. Source attribution was not verified; it is likely Rowland 2014 or a similar review — [Retrievalpractice.org meta-analysis guide](https://pdf.retrievalpractice.org/MetaAnalysisGuide.pdf)
- Yang et al. (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*): 222 classroom studies, more than 48,000 students. Quizzing raises achievement by g = 0.499 overall and g = 0.328 [0.085, 0.571] in elementary school, which is smaller than at other levels — [ResearchGate: Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349906849_Testing_quizzing_boosts_classroom_learning_A_systematic_and_meta-analytic_review); [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-)
- Agarwal et al. (2021) review of retrieval practice in applied settings: of 49 effect sizes, 57% showed medium or large benefits — [Agarwal et al. 2021, EDPR](https://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_etal_2021_EDPR.pdf)
- Elementary-school children show robust retrieval-practice effects in lab-to-classroom studies (Karpicke et al.) — [PMC: Retrieval-Based Learning in elementary school children](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4786565/)
- Spacing in verbal learning: Wiseheart et al. (2019) estimate the effect at d = 0.85 from two meta-analyses. An L2 spaced-practice meta-analysis found d = 0.54 [0.31, 0.77]. Effects were larger with longer retention intervals, higher education levels (older learners) and fewer re-exposures — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757262/full); [ResearchGate: Spaced practice in L2 meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358406370_The_Effects_of_Spaced_Practice_on_Second_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis)
- Spacing works in children's classroom vocabulary learning. One-week spacing beat massed learning for long-term retention in middle-school vocabulary (Sobel et al.), and in young EFL learners — [ResearchGate: Spacing Effects in Real-World Classroom Vocabulary Learning](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227701922_Spacing_Effects_in_Real-World_Classroom_Vocabulary_Learning); [T&F: Spacing effects in vocabulary learning: Young EFL learners](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2017.1287391)
- Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell (2008, *Cognition*): spacing category exemplars over time improved young children's memory and generalisation compared with massing — [Vlach et al. 2008 PDF](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer_Kornell_2008.pdf)
- Vlach et al. (2014): expanding and equal spacing schedules did not differ at an immediate test. At a 24-hour delay, children on the **expanding** schedule significantly outperformed those on the equally spaced schedule — [Vlach 2014 brief report](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2014-vlach.pdf); [PMC version](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3995866)
- Spacing also helped children acquire and generalise science concepts (Vlach & Sandhofer 2012) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3399982/)
- Retrieval practice was examined for word learning in children with developmental language disorder, including whether expanding retrieval adds benefit — [PMC11087082](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11087082/)

### Inferences
- An SRS core is well supported for character and word retention at age 7–8, provided items are actually **retrieved** (recall or production) and not just re-exposed.
- Expect real-world effects of roughly 0.3 SD, not lab-sized 0.6–0.8.
- Effects show at delay. Immediate in-session accuracy is a poor indicator of learning, so measure retention 1+ days later.
- Expanding intervals look reasonable for children. The exact optimal gaps for 7–8-year-olds are not established; day-scale gaps (1 day, then several days, then weeks) are consistent with the classroom vocabulary studies.

### Gaps
- No child-specific evidence was found on optimal SRS intervals or algorithms (e.g. SM-2 or FSRS parameters) for children. The parameters are adult-derived.
- I did not retrieve Rowland (2014) directly. Its g ≈ 0.50 figure is from memory and unverified here.
- No studies were found on spacing or retrieval for Chinese characters with children.

## 2. Interleaving vs blocking and desirable difficulties: limits for young children

### Takeaway
Interleaving helps when learners must discriminate between **similar** categories (e.g. look-alike characters). Blocking can be better for low-similarity categories and for first exposure. Children benefit, but less than adults. **Evidence is moderate for older learners and limited for 7–8-year-olds.**

### Cited Findings
- Interleaving improves learning of high-similarity categories by increasing between-category comparison. Blocking improves low-similarity categories by increasing within-category comparison (Carvalho & Goldstone) — [Springer: Putting category learning in order](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0); [PMC4141442](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4141442/)
- Children and young adults learning natural (rock) categories: interleaving significantly improved categorisation accuracy, and **young adults benefited more than children** (2025, *J. Intelligence*) — [PMC12470837](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12470837/)
- Kang's review: most evidence favours interleaved over blocked training, "although there are noteworthy boundary conditions" — [Kang, Interleaved training and category learning](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-interleaved-training-and-category-learning-kang.pdf)
- Spacing and interleaving effects need distinct theoretical explanations, so their benefits do not automatically transfer to each other (systematic review) — [Springer EDPR 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09613-w)
- Whether interleaving or blocking is better for long-term learning depends on the learner's strategy (2025) — [MDPI Behav Sci](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/5/662)

### Inferences
- Introduce new characters or words in small blocks first, then mix them into interleaved review. Mixing visually or phonetically confusable items is where interleaving should pay off most.
- Desirable difficulty is only "desirable" if the child can succeed with effort. For a 7–8-year-old, too much difficulty risks frustration and guessing; see section 4.

### Gaps
- No child studies were found on interleaving for vocabulary or characters specifically.
- No sourced evidence was found on how much "difficulty" 7–8-year-olds tolerate before disengaging.

## 3. Dual coding, multimedia, segmenting, cognitive load and the redundancy effect

### Takeaway
For young children, picture-plus-narration stories without an adult beat traditional story materials without an adult, for both vocabulary and comprehension. Interactive extras (hotspots, games inside the story) **reduce** learning, consistent with cognitive load. Redundancy (identical text + narration + pictures shown at once) is a risk mostly established with adults. **Evidence: moderate for child multimedia stories; limited/adult-derived for the redundancy effect.**

### Cited Findings
- Takacs, Swart & Bus (2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*): meta-analysis of 29 studies, 1,272 children. Multimedia stories were more beneficial than traditional story materials without adult help, for story comprehension and vocabulary — [PMC4253734](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4253734/)
- Takacs et al. (2015) meta-analysis of technology-enhanced storybooks: interactive features, **whether or not relevant to the story**, decreased the benefits of electronic stories, probably by making children switch tasks and causing cognitive overload — [ResearchGate: Benefits and Pitfalls of Multimedia and Interactive Features](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276334037_Benefits_and_Pitfalls_of_Multimedia_and_Interactive_Features_in_Technology-Enhanced_Storybooks_A_Meta-Analysis); [Takacs dissertation, Leiden](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2872127/view)
- Redundancy principle: presenting the same information through several media at the same time can inhibit learning. Storybooks with written text, narration and pictures of the same content may restrict vocabulary and comprehension gains — [WSU dissertation on multimedia storybooks](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/view/pdfCoverPage?instCode=01ALLIANCE_WSU&filePid=13350067550001842&download=true)
- Yue, Bjork & Bjork (2013), with adults: a **reduced** (abridged) on-screen text alongside narration can help rather than hurt, which qualifies the redundancy effect — [Yue et al. 2013](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/YueBjorkBjork2013_redundancy.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pair each character or word with a meaningful picture and native-speaker audio (dual coding), and keep decorative animation and in-task "fun" interactions out of the learning moment.
- For a child learning to read, the text **is** the learning target, so "redundant" text is not truly redundant. Highlighting text in sync with narration is reasonable. Avoid extra simultaneous captions or long text blocks beyond the target.
- Segment content into short units the child advances through at their own pace.

### Gaps
- No child-specific redundancy-effect experiments were retrieved.
- No evidence was found on segment length for 7–8-year-olds.
- No studies were found on dual coding for hanzi (e.g. pictographic mnemonics), though this is a common practice.

## 4. Feedback, errorful learning, guessing, and making sure practice shows real learning

### Takeaway
Feedback works (d ≈ 0.48 overall), and richer, more informative feedback works much better than bare right/wrong. Errorful generation and pretesting can help, but mostly in adult studies, and there is a catch: errorful generation improves memory for **items, not associations**. For children, multiple-choice recognition overstates learning. **Evidence: strong for feedback in general (mixed ages); limited for errorful learning in children.**

### Cited Findings
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020): 435 studies, 994 effect sizes, more than 61,000 participants. Overall d = 0.48, with large heterogeneity. High-information feedback (including self-regulation information) was most effective (d = 0.99), and cognitive outcomes were d = 0.51. This is lower than Hattie & Timperley's earlier 0.70–0.79 — [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/)
- Pretesting (errorful generation) versus posttesting (retrieval practice) has been compared directly (Pan & Sana 2021, adults); pretesting helps when followed by the correct answer — [Pan & Sana 2021](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PS_2021.pdf)
- "Errorful generation improves memory for items, not associations" (*J. Memory & Language*, 2018). This matters for word–meaning and character–sound pairs, which are associations — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X18300858)
- Effects of errorful generation on recognition memory depend on motivation and surprise — [T&F Memory 2019](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09658211.2019.1647247)
- A dissertation on generating errors and later learning/generalisation, including child samples (not read in full) — [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/105254/bitstreams/333248/data.pdf)
- Kim et al. (2021) found that app effects measured with researcher-made outcomes were about **0.42 SD larger** than those measured with standardised tests. Gains on narrow app tasks overstate transferable learning — [AERA Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23328584211004183)

### Inferences
- Always show the correct answer immediately after an error. For children this age, feedback should be immediate and explanatory, not just a red X. Examples: replay the audio, show the picture, highlight the distinguishing stroke or component.
- Treat multiple-choice correctness as weak evidence, because guessing among 4 options gives 25% chance. Count an item as "known" only after free recall or production (say it, write or trace it, type pinyin, or pick from many distractors) across separate days.
- Detect guessing through response time (implausibly fast) and through pattern streaks. Require a retry after an error before advancing.
- Pretesting or "guess first" is not well evidenced for 7–8-year-olds learning arbitrary character–sound links. Use it sparingly, if at all.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on immediate vs delayed feedback restricted to children was retrieved. Classic findings (immediate is better for young or low-prior-knowledge learners in applied settings) are unverified here.
- No child studies on multiple-choice guessing inflating vocabulary scores were retrieved.

## 5. Metacognition, self-regulation, parental involvement, and how much autonomous app learning a 7–8-year-old can sustain

### Takeaway
Adult involvement consistently increases the benefit of literacy activities, and social interaction is one of the "four pillars" of educational apps. Self-regulation-oriented feedback has the largest feedback effect. However, I found **no direct evidence** quantifying how long a 7–8-year-old can sustain self-paced app learning. **Evidence: moderate that adult involvement helps; insufficient on autonomous session limits.**

### Cited Findings
- Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*): learning is supported when apps are active, engaged, meaningful and socially interactive, within a supported learning goal. Caregiver responsiveness and turn-taking predict language development — [PSPI PDF](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2015/09/Psychological-Science-in-the-Public-Interest-2015-Hirsh-Pasek-3-34.pdf); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100615569721)
- Content analysis of app stores using the Four Pillars: most "educational" apps for young children score low — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349557693_How_educational_are_educational_apps_for_young_children_App_store_content_analysis_using_the_Four_Pillars_of_Learning_framework)
- Therrien (2004) on repeated reading: **adult tutors produced larger fluency effects than peer tutors**, and modelling the passage first increased effects — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249835323_Fluency_and_Comprehension_Gains_as_a_Result_of_Repeated_Reading_A_Meta-Analysis); [ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/903ec865-92fd-4ca5-9cea-8534b161420a)
- Wisniewski et al. (2020): feedback containing self-regulation information was the most effective type (d = 0.99) — [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/)
- Gilbert, Young & Kim (2026): app **dosage did not predict effect sizes** — [EdWorkingPapers ai26-1579](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1579)
- A study of home learning apps found that apps used at home helped prepare children for school — [PMC11868692](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11868692/)

### Inferences
- Design for a parent-in-the-loop. Ideas: a brief parent view of what was learned and what is shaky, prompts for the parent to quiz or talk about the words, and occasional shared read-aloud.
- Simple metacognitive supports fit this age: "Which ones were hard?", showing progress on specific items, and letting the child choose to review tricky ones. Do not rely on children's own judgement of what they know; children over-estimate, though this claim is not sourced in this round.
- Because more dosage did not mean more effect, short daily sessions (about 10–15 minutes, a practical norm, not evidence-derived) spread over many days fit both spacing and attention limits better than long sessions.

### Gaps
- No sourced data were found on sustained attention or session length in self-paced apps for 7–8-year-olds.
- No sourced data were found on how accurately children of this age judge their own learning; JOL accuracy literature in children was not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of parental-involvement effect sizes for app-based learning was retrieved.

## 6. Motivation: gamification, rewards, streaks and mascots

### Takeaway
Tangible, expected, engagement-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, **more for children than adults** (strong evidence: Deci et al. 1999). Gamification has moderate positive effects overall. Points, badges and status mechanics alone do less than challenge, meaningful goals and narrative. **Evidence: strong on the undermining effect; moderate on gamification (mostly older students); none found on streaks or mascots specifically for children.**

### Cited Findings
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), meta-analysis of 128 experiments: tangible rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation. For children, engagement-contingent rewards gave d = −0.46 [−0.55, −0.37]. Tangible rewards were **more detrimental for children** than for college students, and verbal praise was less enhancing for children — [Deci et al. 1999 PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-Deci-Koestner/8ad9801baea65b40fbbe6fc56e34b2b7be47d0ba)
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (2001) re-examined these effects specifically for education — [ERIC EJ642243](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ642243)
- The earlier opposing view (Cameron & Pierce 1994) was contradicted by the 1999 meta-analysis — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-Deci-Koestner/8ad9801baea65b40fbbe6fc56e34b2b7be47d0ba)
- Sailer & Homner (2020) gamification meta-analysis: cognitive g = 0.49 [0.30, 0.69] (k = 19); motivational g = 0.36; behavioural g = 0.25. The search summary also states that reward and status mechanics alone were less effective than designs with challenge, goals and narrative. **This moderator detail was not verified in the primary text** — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://d-nb.info/1202307655/34); [ERIC EJ1245270](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)
- Kim et al. (2021): app effects were about 0.17 SD larger on constrained skills (letters, decoding) than unconstrained skills (vocabulary, comprehension) — [AERA Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23328584211004183)

### Inferences
- Avoid making the reward the goal. Concrete rules:
  - Don't give tangible or redeemable prizes for time spent ("engagement-contingent").
  - Prefer informational feedback about competence, e.g. "You can now read 120 characters; these 5 got stronger."
- Streaks are a form of engagement-contingent reward and a persuasive-design pattern. If used, make them forgiving (rest days, no loss aversion or guilt) and tie them to learning, not time. This is an inference; there is no child streak evidence.
- Mascots and narrative may support the "meaningful/engaged" pillar if they do not interrupt learning moments; see the interactive-features findings in section 3.
- Support autonomy (choices), competence (achievable challenge, visible mastery) and relatedness (parent and teacher sharing), per self-determination theory.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence was found on streaks, mascots or badges for 7–8-year-olds.
- I could not verify the Sailer & Homner element-level moderator claims in the primary text.

## 7. Evidence on educational apps for early literacy; screen-time guidance

### Takeaway
Educational apps for preschool to grade 3 have modest positive effects: about +0.35 SD for literacy in Kim et al. 2021, and d = 0.40 across 141 studies in the 2026 update. Effects are much smaller on standardised measures, in RCTs and in larger samples, and largest on constrained skills. AAP guidance for ages 6+ is consistent limits and protecting sleep and activity; it sets no fixed hour cap. **Evidence: moderate-to-strong that apps help a bit, with inflated estimates on app-aligned measures.**

### Cited Findings
- Kim, Gilbert, Yu & Gale (2021, *AERA Open*): 36 studies, 285 effect sizes, preschool to grade 3. Overall +0.31 SD; literacy +0.35 [0.13, 0.57]; math +0.29. Standardised outcomes were about 0.42 SD lower than researcher-developed ones. Constrained skills were about 0.17 SD higher than unconstrained — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23328584211004183); [Harvard CEPR PDF](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-01/kim-et-al-2021-measures-matter-a-meta-analysis-of-the-effects-of-educational-apps-on-preschool-to-grade-3-children-s.pdf)
- Gilbert, Young & Kim (2026) "Measures Still Matter", 141 studies and 841 effect sizes: pooled d = 0.40 [0.32, 0.49], τ = 0.37 (large heterogeneity). It replicates larger effects on researcher-developed measures and constrained skills, with **lower effects in RCTs and larger samples**. Dosage and app quality ratings did not predict effects. Evidence at scale and with long-term follow-up remains limited — [EdWorkingPapers](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1579); [Substack summary](https://measuredresponse.substack.com/p/measures-still-matter-what-six-more)
- Touchscreen learning meta-analysis for young children — [PMC6305619](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6305619/)
- Game-based literacy app study in preschool children from diverse backgrounds — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041608024001729)
- AAP (2016) for children 6+: place consistent limits on time and types of media, and make sure media does not displace sleep (8–12 hours), physical activity (1 hour) and other health behaviours. Use a Family Media Use Plan, keep media-free times and places (dinner, bedrooms), and no screens for 1 hour before bed — [AAP Pediatrics 2016](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/138/5/e20162592/60321/Media-Use-in-School-Aged-Children-and-Adolescents); [HealthyChildren](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx)
- The AAP issued a newer policy statement, "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (*Pediatrics* vol. 157, issue 2). Its content could not be fetched (HTTP 403) — [AAP 2025/2026 policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-Policy); see also [AAP News on 5–18 personalisation and balance](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/11928/Media-use-for-5-to-18-year-olds-should-reflect)

### Inferences
- Realistic expectation: an app can add a modest increment to school learning, larger on narrow skills such as character recognition than on broad skills such as reading comprehension or oral vocabulary.
- An app's own progress metrics will overstate gains. Judge outcomes on independent measures, such as school tests or reading unfamiliar text.
- Educational app time fits within AAP guidance if it is bounded and does not displace sleep, exercise or reading with a parent.

### Gaps
- The new AAP Digital Ecosystems policy text could not be retrieved. It may address persuasive design such as streaks, autoplay and rewards; check it directly.
- No app meta-analysis specific to second-language or Chinese literacy in children was found.

## 8. Speaking and oral development: interaction, recasts, read-aloud and repeated reading, ASR

### Takeaway
Repeated reading reliably improves fluency in school-age children (fluency ES ≈ 0.64 overall). It works best with an adult, a model reading first, and a cue to attend to meaning. Responsive interaction underpins language development. ASR-based reading tutors (Project LISTEN) showed fluency gains in small studies. **Evidence: strong for repeated reading; moderate for interaction; limited for ASR tutors, especially for Mandarin child speech.**

### Cited Findings
- Therrien (2004) meta-analysis of repeated reading, ages 5–18. Fluency ES 0.64 and comprehension 0.42 overall; on practised (non-transfer) passages, fluency 0.83 and comprehension 0.67. Cueing both fluency and comprehension beat cueing fluency only; adult tutors beat peers; modelling the passage first helped — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249835323_Fluency_and_Comprehension_Gains_as_a_Result_of_Repeated_Reading_A_Meta-Analysis); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/07419325040250040801)
- Repeated reading for students with reading disabilities: positive effects on fluency, especially at elementary level (39 effect sizes) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282245080_The_Effects_of_Repeated_Reading_on_Reading_Fluency_for_Students_With_Reading_Disabilities_A_Meta-Analysis)
- Repeated reading delivered online to upper-elementary students (2024) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01626434231184879)
- Repeated vs wide reading RCT — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022440516300553)
- Project LISTEN Reading Tutor: ASR listens to children reading aloud and gives spoken and graphical feedback. In a study of 34 Hispanic students (one month classroom vs one month of tutor use at 25 minutes/day), the tutor condition produced significant gains on several fluency measures — [ResearchGate: Tutoring Bilingual Students with an Automated Reading Tutor That Listens](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228364770_Tutoring_Bilingual_Students_with_an_Automated_Reading_Tutor_That_Listens); [The Learning Agency overview](https://the-learning-agency.com/the-cutting-ed/article/teaching-kids-to-read-with-speech-recognition-technology/)
- Child speech remains hard for ASR, an active research area with phoneme-recognition models for children's reading (2025) — [arXiv 2503.04710](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04710); [arXiv 1611.02695](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02695)
- Caregiver responsiveness and conversational turn-taking predict children's language development (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015) — [PSPI PDF](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2015/09/Psychological-Science-in-the-Public-Interest-2015-Hirsh-Pasek-3-34.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use a listen → read along → read alone → re-read loop on short passages. Always model first with native audio, and ask a simple meaning question so the child reads for meaning, not just speed.
- An app cannot replace conversational interaction. Prompt the parent to listen to the child read and to talk about the content.
- ASR feedback on 7–8-year-olds' Mandarin, especially tones, is likely error-prone. False "wrong" judgements would frustrate the child, so use ASR leniently (e.g. as encouragement or self-recording playback) rather than as a strict judge.

### Gaps
- Recasts were not searched in this round. The L2 interaction/recast meta-analyses (e.g. Mackey & Goo 2007; Lyster & Saito 2010) are known but unverified here.
- No evidence was found on ASR tone or pronunciation feedback for children learning Mandarin.
- No sourced evidence was found on whether recorded-voice playback (self-listening) improves child pronunciation.
